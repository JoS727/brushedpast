The Quiet Revival of Figurative Painting

After decades of conceptual dominance, a generation of artists is returning to the body — but with a new vocabulary. Inside the studios reshaping how we see the human form.

By Elena Marchetti ● August 4, 2026 ● 12 min read

The painter's studio, photographed during a morning visit. All images by the author unless otherwise noted.

For most of the last forty years, the art world treated figurative painting the way a city treats its old train station: something to acknowledge, occasionally admire, but ultimately work around. The real action, we were told, was elsewhere — in conceptual gestures, in institutional critique, in the expanding field of what art could be. Painting people was, if not quite embarrassing, then certainly not where serious attention was directed.

Something has shifted. Walk into almost any graduate studio in the last three years and you will find someone painting the human figure — not as an exercise, not as nostalgia, but as a deliberate, considered choice. The revival is quiet in the sense that it has arrived without a manifesto, without a gallery's marketing push, without a critic declaring a movement. But it is unmistakable.

"I stopped trying to prove painting was still relevant. I just started painting what I couldn't say any other way — which turned out to be people." — Lia Sørensen, painter, interviewed in her Berlin studio

What the New Figuration Is Not

It is important to say what this revival is not. It is not a return to academic realism. The artists driving it are not interested in replicating what a camera already does better. They are not attempting to resurrect the 19th-century salon, and they are broadly suspicious of any language that frames representation as a political reaction to abstraction.

What they share is something harder to name: a conviction that the body remains the densest site of meaning available to a painter, and that the tools of painting — the push and pull of figure and ground, the temperature of color, the weight of a brushmark — can still carry what words and photographs cannot.

The Vocabulary That Changed

The difference between this generation and the figurative painters of the eighties is mostly a difference in vocabulary. The Neo-Expressionists painted the figure as gesture — as a mark that happened to describe a body. The painters emerging now treat the figure as a site of inquiry. They are asking questions about identity, about memory, about the porous boundary between self and representation, and they are using the slow, deliberate language of oil paint to do it.

"The body is the one subject that refuses to be solved."

This is why so much of the new figurative work reads as simultaneously intimate and strange. The paintings are often small — a deliberate refusal of the spectacle scale that dominated the 2000s. They are often of people the artist knows. And they are frequently painted from memory or from composite reference, not from life or from a single photograph, which gives them a quality of being both specific and slightly unsettled.

Three Painters to Watch

Lia Sørensen (Berlin) paints friends and family in domestic interiors that are never quite settled. A chair is always slightly too large. A window lets in light from an impossible angle. The figures inhabit these spaces the way we inhabit dreams — as if the rules of the room are known to everyone except the viewer.

Tomás Herrera (Mexico City) works at a larger scale, painting groups — families, classroom, a crowd at a market — with a looseness that dissolves into abstraction at the edges of the canvas. The centers hold; the margins don't. It's a strategy that makes the social feel unstable, which may be the point.

Aisha Bello (Lagos / London) paints single figures against fields of saturated color. Her subjects stare directly out of the canvas, meeting the viewer's gaze with an expression that is difficult to read as any single emotion. The paintings are technically precise in the face and dissolve into raw paint everywhere else, a contrast that produces an effect not unlike being looked at.

Why Now

Every shift in art has multiple causes, and it's always tempting to reach for the most convenient explanation. The pandemic made us lonely; we wanted to see people again. There's truth in that, but it's insufficient. The turn toward figuration began before 2020, and it has continued after the social conditions that supposedly produced it have eased.

A better explanation may be simpler. The conceptual turn in art was, at its root, an argument about what counted as art's proper subject. That argument has been won — or, more accurately, it has been exhausted. There is no longer an avant-garde position to stake out against representation. The field is open, and a generation of painters who came of age after the culture wars, after the theory wars, after the media wars, has discovered that the figure is still there, still demanding to be looked at, still capable of surprising the person who paints it.

"I think we got tired of clever. I think we wanted something that would push back." — Tomás Herrera, in conversation, March 2026

None of this means figuration will dominate the next decade. Art doesn't work that way; the moment something becomes a trend, the most interesting artists are usually already moving somewhere else. But it does suggest that the long dismissal of figure painting as a conservative or backward-looking practice was always more of an article of faith than a critical argument — and that faith, it turns out, was not shared by the people actually making the work.

The quiet revival may stay quiet. Or it may be the beginning of something the institutions haven't caught up to yet. Either way, the studios are full, the canvases are wet, and the bodies on them are looking back.
