The Quiet Violence of Underpainting

Brushed Past | August 20, 2026

Every painting begins with a lie. You lay down a wash of burnt sienna or raw umber, a muddy stain across white canvas, and you tell yourself this is just a sketch. A placeholder. The real painting starts when the real colors arrive. But that first layer, the one you rush through on a Tuesday evening, is doing more work than you think.

Underpainting is the skeleton of a piece. It establishes value structure, temperature shifts, and the tonal architecture that every subsequent layer leans against. When painters skip it, or treat it as decoration, the finished work feels unanchored. Colors float. Shadows lack gravity. The eye catches something off but cannot name it. What it is sensing is the absence of that buried structure.

The old masters knew this. Rubens laid down brown-and-gray underpaintings that showed through his glazes like bone beneath skin. Vermeer built his interiors over cool gray-brown foundations, then warmed them with thin layers of ultramarine. The underpainting was never meant to disappear. It was meant to be felt, not seen.

Why We Rush It

Modern painting culture undervalues patience. Social media rewards the finished surface, not the buried process. A time-lapse of a completed portrait gets shares. A photo of a muddy brown underpainting gets silence. So painters skip the foundation stage, chasing the dopamine of visible progress.

I have been guilty of this. Last spring I started a portrait and went straight to local color, skipping the value study. The skin tones fought each other as a group. The cheek was too warm, the forehead too cool. I scraped it back to bare canvas and started over with a monochrome umber pass. Twenty minutes of underpainting fixed what two hours of fiddling could not.

The underpainting is the argument the painting makes before it speaks.

Choosing a Method

There are three broad approaches. A monochrome underpainting uses a single pigment, usually burnt sienna or raw umber, diluted to different values. It maps light and shadow with no commitment to final color. This is the safest entry point for beginners because it isolates one variable: value.

A complementary underpainting lays the opposite color beneath the intended final layer. Green under red skin tones, violet under yellow sunlight. This is harder to control but produces livelier surfaces.

A color-blocked underpainting works the full palette at reduced saturation, establishing color relationships early. It is the most ambitious because it makes decisions about temperature before any detail exists. Done well, the final painting feels inevitable. Done poorly, it locks in mistakes that haunt every layer above.

What the Underpainting Knows

Here is the thing nobody tells you. The underpainting is not preparation for the painting. It is the painting, in its most honest form. Strip away color and detail and what remains is the structural truth of the composition. If the underpainting is weak, no amount of surface polish will save it. If it is strong, the painting nearly finishes itself.

I think about this when I see beginners obsessing over brush brands while their value structure collapses. They are decorating a house with no foundation. Walk inside and the floors are uneven.

Spend the time on the first layer. Let it be ugly and rough. Then build on top with confidence, knowing the structure beneath can hold whatever you put on top.

The finished painting is just the underpainting wearing better clothes.
