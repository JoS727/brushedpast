Why Underpainting Still Matters in 2026

Brushed Past · August 20, 2026

Every generation of painters rediscovers the underpainting. It is one of those techniques that never quite goes away because the logic behind it is structural, not stylistic. You lay down a thin, monotone layer of paint to establish value before you ever touch color. That is it. The simplicity is the point.

The practice goes back centuries. Flemish oil painters built their luminous skin tones over burnt umber and raw sienna grounds. The Venetians worked warm red-brown foundations that glowed through subsequent layers. Even the Impressionists, who publicly rebelled against academic procedure, quietly toned their canvases before laying in those broken color strokes. The underpainting was always there, doing its quiet work underneath the spectacle.

What it gives you is a value map. Before you get seduced by hue, you solve the problem of light and dark. Where does the form turn? Where is the core shadow? Where does reflected light bounce into the underside? These are questions that become harder to answer once color enters the conversation because color carries its own emotional weight. A bright cadmium yellow pulls your eye regardless of whether it sits in the right value relationship. Working monochromatically first lets you see the architecture of the scene without that interference.

There is also a practical benefit that working painters understand intimately. An underpainting dries fast. A thin wash of diluted burnt umber or ultramarine blue sets up in minutes, not days. You can rub into it, wipe it back, lift highlights with a rag or a dry brush. It is forgiving in a way that thick color is not. You are building a skeleton that the flesh of the painting will eventually cover, and the skeleton does not need to be beautiful. It needs to be structurally sound.

The most common starting point is a burnt umber wipe. Thin the paint with mineral spirits or a fast-drying medium to a consistency close to ink. Cover the whole canvas in a warm tone, then wipe away the lights with a rag or a stiff brush. What remains is a monochrome study in warm brown that establishes your composition and value structure in a single pass. It is fast, it is loose, and it forces you to commit to decisions early without locking you into them permanently.

Cool underpaintings work too, and they produce a different emotional result. A thin ultramarine gray ground gives subsequent warm colors a sense of depth and atmosphere. Skies painted over a cool dried layer tend to vibrate with a luminosity that warm-on-warm simply cannot match. This is the complementary temperature contrast that the Venetians exploited so effectively. The underpainting does not have to be invisible in the final piece. Letting traces of it show through is a deliberate aesthetic choice that gives the work historical weight.

Acrylic painters have their own version of this. A fast-drying gesso tinted with fluid acrylic creates the same kind of toned ground without the wait. You can be painting in full color within twenty minutes of laying down the wash. For artists working in sessions rather than continuous studio days, that speed matters. The acrylic essentials kit is a reasonable starting point if you are building out a supply list and want reliable pigments that handle this approach well.

Digital painters lose the physicality of the wipe-back but the logic transfers. Starting a piece on a single layer in grayscale, blocking in values, and only then introducing color on separate layers is the same principle expressed in software. The constraint forces the same discipline. Solve value first. Solve color second. It works because human perception prioritizes value over hue. We read form from lightness and darkness long before we parse what color something is. A painting with strong value structure and weak color will always read better than a painting with gorgeous color and muddy values.

The temptation in 2026 is to skip steps. Generative tools can produce a finished-looking image in seconds. Image-to-image pipelines can lay down a composition instantly. But the painters who still matter, the ones whose work holds up on a wall for years, are the ones who understand that the underneath matters. The underpainting is the closest thing painting has to a foundational truth. It is the step where you are honest with yourself about what the painting actually is before you start performing what you want it to appear to be.

None of this is romantic. It is craft. The craft of building a painting from the ground up, literally, so that everything stacked on top has somewhere solid to stand. Skip it and the surface may still look fine. Do it and the surface has a reason to exist.
