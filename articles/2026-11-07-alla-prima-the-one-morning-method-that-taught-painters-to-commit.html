Alla Prima: The One-Morning Method That Taught Painters to Commit

November 7, 2026 · Technique

Somewhere in the 1860s, painters began finishing paintings the way one finishes breakfast: in one sitting, while the whole thing was still fresh. The Italian phrase is alla prima — "at the first" — though the French called it premier coup, the first stroke, which is truer to the spirit. Instead of the classical schedule of underpainting, drying weeks, and glazings, the painter composes, draws, blocks, models, and refines in wet paint on wet paint, in a race against the drying of the medium itself. Sargent, Manet, and later the Impressionists were not merely painting outdoors because paint tubes had been invented. They were working wet-into-wet because it produces a look nothing else can: a surface where every decision is still visible, still one layer thick, still carrying the exact pressure of the hour it was made in.

The method is demanding precisely because it removes the safety net. With indirect painting, an error costs you patience: scrape back, let it dry, try again next Tuesday. With alla prima, an error costs you the morning, and there is no second coat with which to bury the evidence. That is why the one-sitting portrait is the classic alla prima genre — the sitter's patience is the timer, the light is moving, and the painter must commit while the paint is workable. Sargent's celebrated portraits look effortlessly decisive, and they are, but the decisiveness was rehearsed: studies, charcoal cartoons, and an intimate knowledge of where the paint had to go before the first touch landed. Directness is not the absence of preparation. It is preparation compressed into single-shot confidence.

The practical craft matters most here, and it starts with the fat-over-lean rule, obeyed even within a single session. Open with a fast, thin block-in on a toned ground — solvent-thinned earth colors, no white, laying in the largest shapes and the shadow pattern in about ten minutes. Let those shapes find each other while everything is still movable; a thin block-in stays receptive to correction. Then the paint gets progressively thicker and fatter as the session advances, because fat paint over lean paint dries without cracking, and within one day's work the rule does more work than any talent. Painters who load fat passages at the start are effectively freezing the painting early — the rest of the morning then consists of pushing dense paint around, which reads as rework from across the room.

The second discipline is edge control, which wet-into-wet handles like no other method. Where a wet stroke meets a wet neighbor, the boundary can be left soft by simply letting the colors kiss, or sharpened by waiting a moment for the under-layer to set. This is a full vocabulary: lost edges in the shadows of a cheekbone, half-lost edges along fabric, one single hardest edge somewhere near the focal point. The old instruction to "paint the edges last" is really an instruction to spend your drying window strategically. And because every stroke stays on the surface — no translucent glazes, no buried corrections — the final picture is a diary of decisions: a color note made confidently at 10:15 sits next to one that was hedged at 11:30, and the honest ones age better.

For a working painter, the fastest route into the method is the daily one-hour still life, and it should be hard-scoped. One setup, one light, one session, one surface not larger than 8 by 10 inches. Tonal ground, thin block-in, then work large-to-small: shadow pattern first, then lights, then accents, then edges, stopping when the hour is up whether it is finished or not. Ninety mornings of this will do more for color judgment than years of careful indirect painting, for a simple reason: in the one-sitting method, color decisions are made against the actual light, in one continuous visual conversation, rather than ratified in separate sessions against a dead surface that has lost the day. You are training decisiveness under observation, which is the real skill that alla prima has always taught.

And that is the deeper argument for the method. Alla prima is not a style; it is a stance toward the painting session — that the first correct judgment, made once, with full attention, is better than the tenth judgment made with the previous nine there to hedge against. It is why the method survived the extinction of everything around it: the Impressionists took it outdoors, Sargent took it into the salon, and today's oil sketchers, gouache painters, and plein-air watercolorists are all working some variant of the same contract with themselves. Commit once, watch the paint, and let the surface keep the receipt. The paintings that come out of that contract are not always the most polished in the room. They are the most alive, and audiences have never once mistaken the difference.
