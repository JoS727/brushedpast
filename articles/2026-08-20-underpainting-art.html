The Art of Underpainting

Brushed Past / August 20, 2026 / Art

Every painting starts before the first visible brushstroke. Underpainting is the skeleton beneath the skin, the architecture that holds everything up. It is the quiet conversation between artist and canvas that the viewer never hears but always feels.

Old masters knew this. Titian started with earth tones. Rubens laid down warm browns before color ever touched his panels. The underpainting established values, the dark and light map of the entire composition, so that every layer of color afterward could be applied with confidence rather than guesswork. The painting was already alive before it was colorful.

"A painting is never finished, it only stops in interesting places." The same could be said of underpaintings. They are paintings in their own right, complete compositions rendered in a single tone.

There are two main approaches. The first is grisaille, painting in shades of gray like a black-and-white photograph. You establish every shadow, every highlight, every transition of form using nothing but value. The second is using a single warm or cool earth color, raw umber or burnt sienna, which lets you block in composition while also setting a tonal temperature for the work.

Both approaches do the same thing: they force you to solve the painting's structural problems before you complicate them with color. Value does the heavy lifting in representational art. Get the values right and the color can be loose, even wrong, and the painting still reads. Get the values wrong and no amount of gorgeous color will save it.

This is why beginners struggle. They reach for color first, drawn by the seductive promise of a full palette, and the painting collapses into mud because the underlying structure was never established. It is like building a roof before the walls. Color is decoration on top of form. Form is the form.

The process is straightforward but requires patience. You thin your paint with solvent, not medium, because the underpainting needs to be lean. Fat over lean is not just a rule about archival longevity. It is about how paint behaves. A thin, solvent-rich layer dries fast and matte. A thick, oil-rich layer stays wet and glossy. You want your first layer dry and receptive, a surface that the next layer can grip onto rather than slide across.

Start with a thinned wash of your chosen earth color. Block in the big shapes. Do not paint details. Do not paint eyes or window frames or individual leaves. Paint the light side of the face and the shadow side of the face. Paint the sky as one shape and the ground as another. Squint at your subject until everything simplifies into two or three values: light, mid, dark. That is your underpainting.

Let it dry. This is the hardest part for impatient painters, which is most of them. You want to keep working. But the underpainting needs to set up so the next layer does not lift it. A few hours for a thin wash in warm weather, overnight if you want to be safe. You can speed things along with a drying medium, but the waiting is part of the discipline. It is time spent looking at what you have made and planning what comes next.

When you return, the canvas already has depth. The ghost of the painting is there, rendered in warm brown or cool gray, and now your job is to bring it to life with color. You will find that you need less paint than you think. The underpainting does half the work of every brushstroke you lay on top of it. A warm area in the underpainting shows through a cool color on top, creating a vibration that no single layer can produce. This is optical mixing, and it is what gives oil paintings their characteristic glow.

Underpainting is not limited to oil. Watercolorists use it with their own vocabulary, laying in pale washes of a single color to establish the lightest lights and map the composition before building toward darks. Acrylic painters use it because acrylic dries in minutes, making the technique almost frictionless. Digital painters use it, starting in gray scale before adding color layers in overlay or multiply blend modes. The principle transfers across every medium because it is not about the paint. It is about the thinking.

What underpainting really teaches is restraint. The willingness to work in a limited way before working in a full way. The trust that structure will carry expression. The understanding that depth in painting comes from what is underneath, not what is on the surface. These are lessons that extend beyond art into how we build anything that matters.

The next time you see a painting that feels solid, grounded, real in a way you cannot quite articulate, look at the shadows. Chances are there is an underpainting beneath them, doing its quiet work, invisible and essential. The best parts of any painting are the ones you never see.
